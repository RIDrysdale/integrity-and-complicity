# Tensions

Disagreements between assigned readings that the program does not resolve. An entry appears here only once readings the reader actually encounters instantiate it.

## T-001 — Refusal and what survives it

**Between:** Plato, *Apology*, and Plato, *Crito*. Module 1.

In the *Apology* Socrates refuses an official demand and treats the danger to himself as beside the point (32a–e). In the *Crito* he refuses to escape a sentence he regards as unjust, and the Laws' speech argues that obligations to the city persist even after it has wronged him (50a–54d).

**Why it matters in the report case.** A manager's demand can be refused while obligations to colleagues remain in force. The reverse also holds: having obligations to colleagues does not by itself make producing the report permissible. Each inference needs its own argument, and neither reading supplies both.

**A reconciliation worth testing, not the entry's answer.** Distinguish being required to *do* wrong from being required to *suffer* wrong. Socrates refuses the first and accepts the second. The difficulty is that the Laws appear to demand more than this: the argument from tacit agreement claims that staying constitutes consent to the terms, which reaches past suffering a wrong into endorsing the arrangement that produced it.

**Left open.** Which obligations survive the rejection of a particular demand, and why. What would have to be true for continued membership to count as agreement. Whether the two dialogues genuinely disagree or answer different questions asked in different circumstances.

## T-002 — Where responsibility is located

**Between:** Rawls, *A Theory of Justice* §§18–19, and Arendt, "Personal Responsibility Under Dictatorship." Module 1.

Rawls locates obligation in a structure: a just scheme, benefits accepted, a position taken up. The particular demand does not enter into it. Arendt treats that same structural framing as the mechanism by which responsibility disappears — the cog defense is an accurate description of bureaucratic reality and still no defense at all.

**Why it matters in the report case.** Rawls gives you an account of why you owe something to colleagues you never chose. Arendt gives you a reason to distrust exactly that account when it arrives in your own mouth as a reason for compliance.

**Left open.** Whether the two are actually opposed. Rawls describes what generates an obligation; Arendt describes how a person evades judging what they are doing. A structure could ground real obligations and still be the place responsibility goes to hide. If so, the reader needs a way to tell which is happening, and neither text supplies one.
