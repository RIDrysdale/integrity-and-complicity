# Tensions

Disagreements between assigned readings that the program does not resolve. An entry appears here only once readings the reader actually encounters instantiate it. A disagreement between one reading and a plausible misuse of another does not qualify. Neither does a difference of route to the same conclusion, nor two texts answering different questions in different vocabularies. The test is that the readings, put to the same case, would say different things about it.

## T-001 — Refusal and what survives it

**Between:** Plato, *Apology*, and Plato, *Crito*. Module 1.

In the *Apology* Socrates refuses an official demand and treats the danger to himself as beside the point (32a–e). In the *Crito* he refuses to escape a sentence he regards as unjust, and the Laws' speech argues that obligations to the city persist even after it has wronged him (50a–54d).

**Why it matters in the report case.** A manager's demand can be refused while obligations to colleagues remain in force. The reverse also holds: having obligations to colleagues does not by itself make producing the report permissible. Each inference needs its own argument, and neither reading supplies both.

**A reconciliation worth testing, not the entry's answer.** Distinguish being required to *do* wrong from being required to *suffer* wrong. Socrates refuses the first and accepts the second. The difficulty is that the Laws appear to demand more than this: the argument from tacit agreement claims that staying constitutes consent to the terms, which reaches past suffering a wrong into endorsing the arrangement that produced it.

**Left open.** Which obligations survive the rejection of a particular demand, and why. What would have to be true for continued membership to count as agreement. Whether the two dialogues genuinely disagree, or answer different questions asked in different circumstances, or give one answer twice.

## T-002 — Whether a duty to disclose is the role-holder's or the rules'

**Between:** Cicero, *De Officiis* III.50–57, and Fried, "The Lawyer as Friend". Module 2.

Cicero's criterion at III.57 is that holding one's peace is not concealment, but that concealment is trying, for your own profit, to keep others from finding out something you know when it is for their interest to know it. He reaches it against Diogenes, whose position was that a seller declares what the law requires and may otherwise sell to best advantage while misrepresenting nothing. The verdict does not turn on what the law required.

Fried holds that a lawyer in a reasonably just system confines himself to the law and the rules of advocacy, and that social policy is made by changing what the law permits rather than by asking the role-holder to curb his advocacy inside it (III.B.1). Taking advantage of an adversary's ignorance in a negotiation is one of the two criticisms he sets out to answer (I.A), and he answers it by locating the correction in the rules rather than in the person applying them.

**Why it matters in the report case.** Nothing in the analyst's report is false. The manager's question is whether they must supply what no rule obliges them to supply. Cicero puts that duty on the person and hands over a test for it. Fried, for a professional inside a system that clears his justice condition, puts it on whoever writes the rules.

**A reconciliation worth testing, not the entry's answer.** Fried draws his own line at wrongs the role-holder personally commits, and Cicero's criterion is a description of a personal wrong. The two may agree about the principle and disagree only about which side a misleading silence falls. The difficulty is that Cicero thinks his formulation settles that and Fried thinks no formulation does.

**Left open.** Whether a duty of disclosure that no rule imposes is owed by the person holding the role. Whether Cicero's two conditions — your own profit, their interest in knowing — are each necessary or only jointly sufficient. Whether an arrangement in which every department discounts what it reads is one where the rules have already answered the question.

## T-003 — Whether how you are treated bears on what you owe

**Between:** Mencius 4B.3 and Fried, "The Lawyer as Friend". Module 2.

Mencius grades the obligation by the superior's conduct: ministers treated as hands and feet respond as belly and heart, those treated as dogs and horses as they would to any ordinary man, those treated as the ground or as grass as to a robber and an enemy. The passage concerns whether a departing minister mourns a ruler whose service he has left, and its continuation makes obstruction of departure the mark of the worst case.

Fried's lawyer owes what he owes irrespective of what the client deserves — the essay is explicit that there is a vocation in helping Shylock to his pound of flesh. The condition Fried does attach runs to the justice of the system issuing the role, not to the conduct of anyone inside the relation.

**Why it matters in the report case.** The manager is now the one whose conduct is being weighed. On the first account, what the analyst owes them is partly a function of how they have been treated, and the manager can forfeit it. On the second it is not, and the only condition that bears is one neither of them controls.

**A reconciliation worth testing, not the entry's answer.** These may be answering different questions — Mencius about what passes between two people in a relation, Fried about a role an institution issues and can withdraw. The difficulty is that the manager occupies both descriptions at once, and the two accounts give different advice about the same Thursday.

**Left open.** Whether an obligation that lapses when the other party behaves badly is an obligation or a bargain. Whether one that never lapses is an obligation or a trap. Whether Fried's condition can be assessed by the person inside the institution who needs the answer.
