# Tensions

Disagreements between assigned readings that the program does not resolve. An entry appears here only once readings the reader actually encounters instantiate it. A disagreement between one reading and a plausible misuse of another does not qualify.

## T-001 — Refusal and what survives it

**Between:** Plato, *Apology*, and Plato, *Crito*. Module 1.

In the *Apology* Socrates refuses an official demand and treats the danger to himself as beside the point (32a–e). In the *Crito* he refuses to escape a sentence he regards as unjust, and the Laws' speech argues that obligations to the city persist even after it has wronged him (50a–54d).

**Why it matters in the report case.** A manager's demand can be refused while obligations to colleagues remain in force. The reverse also holds: having obligations to colleagues does not by itself make producing the report permissible. Each inference needs its own argument, and neither reading supplies both.

**A reconciliation worth testing, not the entry's answer.** Distinguish being required to *do* wrong from being required to *suffer* wrong. Socrates refuses the first and accepts the second. The difficulty is that the Laws appear to demand more than this: the argument from tacit agreement claims that staying constitutes consent to the terms, which reaches past suffering a wrong into endorsing the arrangement that produced it.

**Left open.** Which obligations survive the rejection of a particular demand, and why. What would have to be true for continued membership to count as agreement. Whether the two dialogues genuinely disagree, or answer different questions asked in different circumstances, or give one answer twice.

## T-002 — Whether there is anyone behind the office

**Between:** Cicero, *De Officiis*, and Confucius, *Analects*, with Mencius. Module 2.

Cicero holds that we are invested by nature with two characters before any office exists — one universal, arising from reason shared with everyone, and one particular to the individual — and that office arrives third, imposed by chance or circumstance (I.107, I.115). The verdict against the grain dealer at III.57 is drawn from the first of these.

The *Analects* does not supply that person. Role terms are treated as normative rather than descriptive, so that a ruler can fail to be one (12.11), and personal conduct and the conduct of office are not two things (13.13). *Ren* is not a standard held apart from the relations to be appealed to against them.

**Why it matters in the report case.** The manager wants to appeal from *what a manager must do* to *what a person must do*. Cicero grants the appeal and tells them where to file it. On the other account there is no such court, and any objection to the softened report has to be made in the role's own terms — which is a harder discipline, not a softer one.

**A reconciliation worth testing, not the entry's answer.** Perhaps Cicero's universal *persona* is not a self standing behind the roles but the claim that role terms answer to standards their occupants do not set — which the *Analects* also holds, at 13.3. The difficulty is that Cicero needs the universal *persona* to generate duties directly, as it does at III.57, and not merely to keep role terms honest.

**Left open.** Whether the disagreement is metaphysical or verbal. What evidence could bear on it. Whether an ethics with no role-independent standard can criticise an arrangement that is running smoothly, and where the resources for that criticism come from if it can.

## T-003 — Separation from the office: safeguard or excuse

**Between:** Montaigne, *Essays* III.10, and Confucius, *Analects* 13.13. Module 2.

Montaigne says the Mayor of Bordeaux and Montaigne have always been two, that an honest man is not accountable for the vice or absurdity of his employment, and that the judgment of an emperor ought to stand above his empire and regard it as a foreign accident. The separation is offered as what keeps a judgment capable of finding the office questionable.

*Analects* 13.13 rules it out: a man who cannot make his own person upright has nothing to do with making others upright. There is no second man to hold the first one's judgment in reserve.

**Why it matters in the report case.** *As manager I have to* is the sentence the whole case turns on. Montaigne's separation is what makes that sentence available, and is also aimed squarely at the condition — the officeholder who cannot tell the salutations made to himself from those made to his commission — that makes the sentence corrupt. The same formulation does both jobs.

**A reconciliation worth testing, not the entry's answer.** Distinguish separating your judgment from the office, which is what Montaigne says the practice is for, from separating your accountability from it, which is what the sentence usually accomplishes. The difficulty is that Montaigne's own wording is about accountability and not about judgment.

**Left open.** Whether the judgment-preserving form of the separation can be had without the accountability-shedding form. Whether anything other than the officeholder's own character holds them apart, and what a person who has lost the distinction would notice.

## T-004 — What detachment is for

**Between:** Montaigne, *Essays* III.10, and the *Bhagavad Gita*. Module 2.

Both counsel a kind of distance between the person and the act. Montaigne lends himself to the office and gives himself only to himself, conceding it his attention, pains and blood while keeping his mind out of it. The *Gita* tells Arjuna to hold pleasure and pain, gain and loss, victory and defeat as equal and gird himself for battle (2.38), on the ground that the dweller in the body is never destroyed (2.30).

The two run opposite ways. Montaigne's distance exists to protect a judgment that can still find the office questionable from outside. Krishna's arrives after Arjuna's judgment has been overruled, and what it makes possible is the act that judgment refused.

**Why it matters in the report case.** Detachment is the posture the manager is most likely to adopt and least likely to examine, because it feels like maturity in both versions. Nothing in the posture itself reveals which of the two it is.

**Left open.** Whether detachment from one's own acts is a safeguard against being captured by a role or the solvent that dissolves resistance to it, and whether it can be both at once. What test, applied from inside, would tell a person which they have.

## T-005 — Whether a silence that misleads is a wrong

**Between:** Cicero, *De Officiis* III.50–57, and Fried, "The Lawyer as Friend". Module 2.

Cicero puts the case of the grain dealer at Rhodes who knows that more ships are coming and says nothing, has Diogenes argue that concealing and merely not revealing are different things, and decides against him: the dealer was obliged not to keep the facts back (III.57). The verdict follows from the claim at III.49 that nothing morally wrong is ever expedient.

Fried names the exploitation of an adversary's ignorance in a negotiation as one of the two standing criticisms of the lawyer's role (I.A), and builds an argument that protects much of the space Diogenes was claiming (II.D). He then draws a limit at wrongs the role-holder personally commits as against wrongs a reasonably just system works through its rules (III.B.2) — and declines to say which side this kind of silence falls on.

**Why it matters in the report case.** It is the case. Nothing in the report is false and the misleading is done by what is left out. Cicero answers the question in general and answers it against the manager; Fried denies that it has a general answer and supplies a distinction that could place it either way.

**Left open.** Whether misleading by omission is a personal wrong or something an arrangement works through its rules, and what settles it. Whether a practice in which everyone discounts what they read makes the omission harmless, as the manager's argument claims, or merely makes it universal.

## T-006 — What a role-claim depends on, and whether it can lapse

**Between:** Mencius 4B.3, the *Bhagavad Gita*, and Fried, "The Lawyer as Friend". Module 2.

Mencius grades the obligation by the superior's conduct: ministers treated as hands and feet respond as belly and heart, those treated as dogs and horses respond as to any ordinary man, and those treated as the ground or as grass owe a robber and an enemy nothing at all.

In the *Gita* nothing about Arjuna's duty turns on the conduct of the men across the field. *Svadharma* is fixed by station, and 18.48 adds that no undertaking is free of defect.

Fried makes the lawyer's licence conditional on the justice of the system that issues it (III.B.1), but not at all on the deserts of the client, who may be guilty or may be Shylock.

**Why it matters in the report case.** The manager is now the one being graded. On the first account, what the analyst owes them depends on how they have been treated; on the second it does not depend on anything; on the third it depends on an institution neither of them controls. These give different answers about whether the analyst's refusal is insubordination, a verdict, or beside the point.

**Left open.** Whether a role-obligation that lapses when the institution behaves badly is a real obligation or a bargain. Whether one that never lapses is an obligation or a trap. Whether Fried's condition — a reasonably just system — can be assessed by the person inside it who needs the answer.
