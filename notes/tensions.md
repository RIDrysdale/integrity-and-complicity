# Tensions

Disagreements the reader actually encounters and the program does not resolve. Usually these fall between assigned readings. They may also fall inside one, where a text stages a disagreement between named positions: the reader meets the conflict either way, and which cover it falls between is not what makes it a tension.

An entry appears only once the assigned passages instantiate it. A disagreement between one reading and a plausible misuse of another does not qualify. Neither does a difference of route to the same conclusion, nor two texts answering different questions in different vocabularies. The test is that the positions, put to the same case, would say different things about it.

Where a text settles its own disagreement, an entry must say what survives the settlement and why. Otherwise every dialogue would qualify and the file would mean nothing.

## T-001 — Refusal and what survives it

**Between:** Plato, *Apology*, and Plato, *Crito*. Module 1.

In the *Apology* Socrates refuses an official demand and treats the danger to himself as beside the point (32a–e). In the *Crito* he refuses to escape a sentence he regards as unjust, and the Laws' speech argues that obligations to the city persist even after it has wronged him (50a–54d).

**Why it matters in the report case.** A manager's demand can be refused while obligations to colleagues remain in force. The reverse also holds: having obligations to colleagues does not by itself make producing the report permissible. Each inference needs its own argument, and neither reading supplies both.

**A reconciliation worth testing, not the entry's answer.** Distinguish being required to *do* wrong from being required to *suffer* wrong. Socrates refuses the first and accepts the second. The difficulty is that the Laws appear to demand more than this: the argument from tacit agreement claims that staying constitutes consent to the terms, which reaches past suffering a wrong into endorsing the arrangement that produced it.

**Left open.** Which obligations survive the rejection of a particular demand, and why. What would have to be true for continued membership to count as agreement. Whether the two dialogues genuinely disagree, or answer different questions asked in different circumstances, or give one answer twice.

## T-002 — Whether not revealing is concealing

**Between:** Antipater and Diogenes of Babylonia, as Cicero reports them at *De Officiis* III.51–57. Module 2.

The facts are stipulated and shared, which is what makes this the cleanest disagreement in the module. There is famine at Rhodes and a trader arrives with grain, knowing that several more ships are close behind him and that nobody else knows it.

Antipater holds that everything bearing on the buyer's position must be disclosed, because you were brought into the world to serve the common interest and your advantage is not separable from it. Diogenes holds that the seller declares what the law of the place requires and may otherwise sell to best advantage provided he misrepresents nothing, because it is one thing to conceal and quite another merely not to reveal. At III.54 the case is made harder with a house the seller knows to be unsound, and neither man shifts.

Cicero decides for Antipater at III.57 and supplies a criterion: holding one's peace is not concealment, but concealment is trying, for your own profit, to keep others from finding out what it is in their interest to know.

**Why the disagreement survives that.** The criterion grants Diogenes his distinction rather than brushing it aside — not everything you hold back is concealment — and then draws the line so that the merchant falls on the far side of it. A reader who does not grant where the line is drawn is returned to the start. The criterion also joins two conditions without asking whether either works alone, and cases arise — the manager's among them — that may satisfy the second while the first is genuinely unclear.

**Why it matters in the report case.** The manager's argument is Diogenes': everything stated is true, the practice is known to everyone inside it, and an audience that discounts what it is told has not been deceived. Whether the two cases are the same case is itself contested, and the last paragraph below says why.

**A reconciliation worth testing, not the entry's answer.** Perhaps the two differ only about whether custom can fix what a person is entitled to expect. Diogenes lets the law and usage of the market set that baseline; Antipater denies that usage can make silence honest. The difficulty is that the manager's whole case rests on custom — every department presents its work favourably and leadership discounts accordingly — so whichever way this falls, it falls on them.

**Left open.** Whether Diogenes' distinction can be restated in a form that survives III.57. Whether the criterion's two conditions are each necessary or only jointly sufficient. And whether a report is the merchant's case at all: he owns his cargo and came by his knowledge privately, where the manager neither owns the report nor sought the findings for himself. An antecedent duty to pass on what was commissioned may settle the matter before Cicero's question arises, which would mean the reader has been handed the wrong tool rather than a blunt one.

## T-003 — How far a relation may move you

**Between:** Cicero, *De Officiis* III.43, and Confucius, *Analects* 13.18. Module 2.

This entry is admitted with a qualification the file's own test requires, and the qualification is stated first. The two passages are not the same case answered two ways, and an earlier draft of this entry said they were.

Cicero rules on a judge hearing a friend's case. III.43 opens by making it a breach of duty to fail a friend in what you may rightly do for him, and puts honours, riches and pleasures below friendship. Only then the limit: an upright man will do nothing against his country, his oath or his good faith for a friend's sake, *ne si iudex quidem erit de ipso amico; ponit enim personam amici, cum induit iudicis*. And the sentence after that grants friendship what it still may have — he may wish his friend's side the juster, and may set the hearing at his friend's convenience so far as the law permits. The friendship is excluded from the verdict, not extinguished.

The Duke of She reports an upright man of his district who testified against his father for stealing a sheep. Confucius answers that the upright men of his own district are different: fathers cover up for their sons and sons for their fathers, and uprightness lies therein. No office, oath or official duty appears in the passage, and nothing in it addresses a judge.

**What the pair establishes, and what it does not.** Not that the texts contradict each other on one case; neither speaks to the other's situation. What they establish is a genuine difference about when a relation may move someone who owes an account to other people. Cicero identifies a position from which it may not; Confucius identifies a relation in which uprightness consists. Put to the manager's case, they pull in different directions without either deciding it.

**Why it matters in the report case.** The manager owes an account to people who commissioned the report and stands in relations to the people who wrote it and depend on him. Cicero supplies a reason for keeping the second out of the first; Confucius supplies a reason for thinking the second is where uprightness lives. Neither tells him whether his position is more like a judge's or more like a father's, and the case has been built so that it is not obviously either.

**A complication worth testing, not the entry's answer.** III.57 makes concealment a matter of keeping people ignorant for one's own gain, which raises the question whether it describes the son at all. Two things block the easy conclusion. The Analects does not establish that the son gains nothing — a father protected is a household protected — so disinterest has to be stipulated. And falling outside Cicero's description of concealment is not permission: he says only that not everything withheld is concealment, and an independent duty to testify would be untouched. III.57 and III.43 are separate prohibitions rather than rival tests, and an act may breach one without answering to the other. What survives is a limit on the criterion's reach, not a conflict inside Cicero.

**Left open.** Whether 13.18 reaches past kin, and what a reader may do with it if it does not. Whether anything in Cicero's judge carries to an office that adjudicates nothing. And what governs an act that escapes both of his descriptions — a silence kept for someone else, breaching no oath — which is where a good deal of institutional life happens, the manager's Thursday among it.
