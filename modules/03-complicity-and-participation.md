# Module 3: Complicity and Participation

*What counts as taking part in wrongdoing someone else is doing?*

---

## Reading 1: Thomas Aquinas, *Summa Theologiae* II-II, q. 62 a. 7 and q. 78 a. 4

Freely available, and the question and article numbers work in every edition and translation. New Advent has the 1920 English Dominican translation, which is where the quotations below come from: [Question 62](https://www.newadvent.org/summa/3062.htm) and [Question 78](https://www.newadvent.org/summa/3078.htm). Two articles, half an hour.

Each runs to a fixed shape: objections, a contrary authority, a short answer beginning *I answer that*, then replies to the objections in turn. Read the replies. In both of these they carry more than the answer does.

### Why it's here

Someone takes what is not theirs, and you were somewhere nearby. Aquinas asks who has to give it back.

That is narrower than this module's question, and the narrowness is what makes it useful. He is not asking who is wicked. He is asking who owes the person robbed, which has a determinate answer — and getting to it requires sorting every way of being the cause of a taking you did not carry out. What comes back is a list of nine. The argument for why the nine do not sit in a row is worth more than the list.

The second article turns the question round: not what you did towards a wrong, but what you may do with one already under way and already someone else's.

### What to watch for

**The division that comes before the list.** Aquinas sorts causes into direct and indirect before naming any of them. Direct: inducing the taking, by command, counsel, consent, or by praising a man for his courage in thieving; helping the taker, by shelter "or any other kind of assistance"; or going in on the thing itself as a fellow evil-doer. Indirect: not preventing another's evil-doing — and here a condition arrives that attaches to nothing above it, "provided he be able **and** bound to prevent him."

Then the nine, given as a single summary: "By command, by counsel, by consent, by flattery, by receiving, by participation, by silence, by not preventing, by not denouncing."

**Which of them always bind, and why the reasons differ.** Five bind in every case: command, consent, receiving, participation, and failing to prevent. The reason given for each is a different kind of reason. Command, because the commander is the chief mover. Consent — and it is narrower than the word suggests — only in the sense of one "without whose consent the robbery cannot take place." Failing to prevent, where office requires it, which he grounds in pay: persons in authority owe restitution when thieves prosper by their neglect, "because their salary is given to them in payment of their preserving justice here below."

The rest are conditional. Counsel and flattery "are not always the efficacious cause of robbery," so they bind only where the taking can be judged probably to have followed from them. Failing to denounce or resist a thief binds "only when he is obliged, in virtue of his office, to do so."

Count what is deciding those. Whether you were necessary. Whether your words in fact produced anything. Whether the job you hold requires it. Three unlike tests inside one list, none of them reducible to the others.

Note also what the list is a list of. It sorts causes. Receiving is harbouring the thief; participation is sharing in the deed and in the booty. Someone who simply ends up better off is not on it.

**The commander whose command is refused.** The second objection raises a person who cooperates towards a taking "without its being taken away in effect," and Aquinas concedes the case: when a command is not carried out, no restitution is owed, "since its end is chiefly to restore the property of the person who has been unjustly injured." He also ranks the liable — the commander first, then the executor, then the rest — and if one of them pays, the others owe that victim nothing.

An order that succeeds puts you at the head of the queue. The same order refused puts you nowhere in it. Work out what that tells you about what is being measured here.

**The turn in q. 78 a. 4.** May you borrow from a man who lends at interest, when lending at interest is a sin? "It is by no means lawful to induce a man to sin, yet it is lawful to make use of another's sin for a good end." You may not ask a man to become a usurer; you may borrow from one who already is, provided your end is good. A man fallen among thieves may likewise point out his own property to them, "which they sin in taking," in order to save his life.

Look at what he is answering. The objection is Romans 1:32, that those who consent to wrongdoers are worthy of death alongside them — the same verse he had used in q. 62 a. 7 to establish that consenters owe restitution. One authority, founding liability in one article and set aside in the other, on a distinction: the borrower "does not consent to the usurer's sin but makes use of it."

**Two conditions in the last reply, independent of each other.** Is depositing money with a usurer like leaving a sword with a madman? Aquinas does not deny the principle; he limits it. If the usurer lacks "other means of practising usury," or if you deposit it intending to profit by the usury, you are "giving a sinner matter for sin," a participator in his guilt. If he has other means, depositing it for safekeeping is no sin, "since this is to use a sinner for a good purpose."

Either limb alone is enough. One asks whether you were necessary; the other asks what you meant; neither collapses into the other. And a reply just above adds a consideration belonging to neither — whether the other person's fall comes from malice, or from weakness and ignorance.

### What it pulls against

Readings ahead take up these phenomena and cut them differently. Rather than predicting how, carry three things apart from one another.

The necessity test, which appears twice: in consent without which the theft cannot happen, and in the usurer who has other means. You met that claim in the first module as the cog argument, where Arendt held it true as a description of how things work and no good at all as a defence. Here it is not a defence but a criterion, built into the machinery. Whether it can bear that weight is a separate question from whether it is true.

The office condition, tied to being paid for the job. That is a test you can actually run on a salaried position. It is also one whose answer you supply, since what your job requires is partly your own account of it.

The frame. This is an argument about a debt to the person robbed, measured by their loss. A reading could accept every distinction here and deny that their loss is the right measure, or keep the measure and reject the distinctions.

### Questions to carry

- Put your own position under whichever of the nine fit. More than one will. Say what is different about each, in your own words rather than his.
- Run the necessity test honestly. If central data would produce the extract anyway, are you the man depositing with a usurer who has other means? What would you have to find out before you could answer?
- Then the other limb, which the first does not settle. Suppose you are not necessary and your department still takes its fifth. Aquinas asks what you intended by the deposit, not what you gained from it. Are those the same thing here?
- Is what you said in the corridor counsel? On his test it binds only if the return probably followed from it — and you cannot now reconstruct what you said. What do you do with a test you cannot run?
- Nine named ways of being implicated, most of them conditional, is a fine instrument. It is also a menu. Name the item you would reach for first if you wanted to be told you were clear, and then say whether it is the one that fits.

### What kind of argument this is *(read after the text)*

Casuistry, in the old and non-pejorative sense: principles worked out by running them against particular cases until the exceptions show where they bend. Its strength is that it yields rules with their conditions stated rather than left to judgment. Its claim is not that these nine acts are wrong to nine different degrees, but that they become liabilities for different reasons — so that one person can head the queue under one item and be absent from it under another, which no single scale would show.

Its difficulty is the size of what it sets aside. The question is restitution, a debt owed to a person for a thing. That is why the failed command owes nothing, and why you may point robbers at your own goods. Aquinas says in his first reply that whoever is in any way a cause of the sin is bound as well as the one who commits it, so he is not denying there is more to say. But this article measures the debt, and a reader who wants to know what they have become rather than what they owe will find the instrument silent. That is a limit on how far it carries, and it is an easy one to walk past.
