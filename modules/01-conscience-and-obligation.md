# Module 1: Conscience and Obligation

*What makes an institutional demand binding, and what could justify refusing one?*

Four readings stage a disagreement. Socrates refuses an official order and explains why. The same man then refuses to escape a sentence he believes is unjust, and explains that too. Rawls sets out what could make an institution's claims on you binding in the first place. Arendt examines how a person inside a hierarchy stops judging what they are doing.

By the end you should be able to state the strongest case for refusing an institutional demand, the strongest case for honouring obligations to an institution that has wronged you, and what each case requires in order to work. You are not expected to choose between them, and the module does not conclude that one of them wins.

## What is assigned

- **Plato, *Apology*** — the whole dialogue. Short, roughly an hour.
- **Plato, *Crito*** — the whole dialogue. Shorter still.
- **Rawls, *A Theory of Justice*, §§18–19** — two sections only. The rest of the book is not assigned and is not needed here.
- **Arendt, "Personal Responsibility Under Dictatorship"** — the whole essay. The longest and hardest of the four.

A reasonable pace is one reading per sitting, in the order given. The case comes first.

## The case: the internal report

You are responsible for preparing an internal report. Your manager asks you to remove or soften several unfavorable findings because, in their view, the report is technically accurate but gives senior leadership an unnecessarily negative impression of the team's performance. Nothing you are being asked to write is literally false. You believe the revised report would nevertheless leave a reasonable reader with a materially misleading impression.

Your manager argues that every department presents its work favorably, that leadership already understands how these reports are produced, that your team's budget could be harmed by unnecessary candor, and that several colleagues' jobs may eventually depend on that budget.

You are not being asked to break a law or falsify a number. What do you do?

**Settle on a position before you read.** Fix it clearly enough in your mind that you would notice it changing. Writing it down makes that easier, and you can do this module without writing anything.

- Comply.
- Refuse.
- Negotiate a middle path.
- Leave, or begin planning to.
- I don't know yet. *(Then: what would you actually do on Monday?)*
- Information is missing. *(Then: what, and could you get it by Friday?)*

Note also what you are assuming about your manager. You may be taking them to concede that the softened report misleads and to be trading that against the budget. They may instead be denying that it misleads at all. Those are different conversations.

## Reading 1: Plato, *Apology*

Freely available; the Stephanus numbers below appear in the margins of most editions. [Jowett translation](https://classics.mit.edu/Plato/apology.html).

### Why it's here

Socrates describes two occasions when he refused an official demand, and his explanation is not the one we expect. He does not say that his conscience forbade it, or that he followed his heart. He says that he knows certain acts to be wrong, that doing wrong damages the person who does it, and that this consideration outranks the danger of refusing.

### What to watch for

The two refusals at 32a–e are not the same act. As a presiding officer he used his position to obstruct the collective trial of the generals, which he describes as contrary to law, at direct risk to himself. Under the Thirty he was ordered to help arrest Leon of Salamis; he went home instead, and the others went and arrested Leon anyway.

Notice what Socrates does not claim. He repeatedly denies knowing the large things. His certainty is narrow and concerns particular acts.

Notice his position, and be careful about it. He has no employer, no career, and no institutional future to protect. But he is not a man without stakes: he describes his poverty, and he has three sons, two of them young. The question for us is not whether he had anything to lose. It is whether an argument developed by someone with no ongoing institutional relationship transfers to someone whose livelihood runs through the institution making the demand.

### What it pulls against

The *Crito*, immediately and directly. Also Rawls, who locates obligation in what you have accepted rather than in what an act does to the person doing it.

### Questions to carry

- Leon was arrested regardless. Did Socrates' refusal accomplish anything? If it did not, what made it right?
- If refusing is justified, does anything further get owed to the person left exposed?
- He had dependants and refused anyway. Does that make the refusal more admirable, or does it make it a cost imposed on people who did not choose it?

### What kind of argument this is *(read after the text)*

An agent-centered argument: the emphasis falls on what the act does to the person committing it rather than on the harm caused or the rule broken. Modern descendants travel under *integrity* and *virtue*.

The emphasis is real but the exclusions are not clean. He appeals to law when he describes the trial of the generals, and elsewhere to injury done to the city. Read him as weighting the agent's own conduct heavily, not as denying that consequences and rules bear on the question at all.

## Reading 2: Plato, *Crito*

Freely available. [Jowett translation](https://classics.mit.edu/Plato/crito.html).

### Why it's here

The man who refused the Thirty now refuses to escape a sentence he regards as unjust. Something survived the wrong done to him. This reading asks what.

### What to watch for

The argument arrives in two stages. First, at 49a–e, Socrates gets Crito to agree that one must never do wrong, not even in return for wrong. Everything after depends on that premise. Test it before conceding it.

Then Socrates does something odd: he stops speaking in his own voice and imagines the Laws of Athens arguing with him (50a–54d). Their speech contains at least four separable claims — that escaping would destroy the city (50a–b), that the relation is one of inequality like that between parent and child (50c–51c), that he was obliged either to persuade the city or obey it (51b–c), and that by remaining in Athens all his life he agreed to its terms (51c–52e).

Assess each on its own. Ask what each would require in order to hold, and whether the four stand or fall together. Whether Socrates himself accepts everything the Laws say, or is testing an argument he has put in their mouths, is genuinely disputed.

### What it pulls against

The *Apology*. One possible reconciliation is worth testing: perhaps Socrates refuses to *do* wrong but accepts having wrong *done to him*. See whether the Laws' demands stay inside that distinction, particularly the argument from agreement.

### Questions to carry

- Which of the Laws' four arguments would you accept from an employer? Would you accept the parent analogy? The argument from having stayed?
- If you reject all of them and still believe you owe your colleagues something, where does that obligation come from?
- What would have to be true for continued membership to count as agreement?
- The *Apology* gives you grounds to refuse. Does anything here give you grounds to think refusal is not enough on its own?

### What kind of argument this is *(read after the text)*

Obligation grounded in relationship and acceptance rather than in the content of the demand. The claim is that you are bound because of what you have received and what you have gone along with. Rawls makes this precise; Nozick attacks it.

## Reading 3: John Rawls, *A Theory of Justice*, §§18–19

In copyright and widely held in libraries. The section numbers work in both the original and the revised edition, so any copy will do. Only these two sections are assigned.

### Why it's here

The Laws' agreement argument, stated rigorously and then deliberately narrowed.

Rawls separates two things that ordinary talk runs together. **Obligations** arise from our own voluntary acts. **Natural duties** bind us regardless of anything we have done — the duty not to harm, and the duty to support just institutions among them. The principle of fairness belongs to the first kind: where a just scheme of cooperation exists, and you have voluntarily accepted its benefits or taken up a position within it, you are bound to do your part rather than free-ride on everyone who does.

The narrowing is the interesting part. Rawls holds that most citizens do not acquire political obligation this way at all, because being born somewhere is not a voluntary act. They are bound instead by the natural duty of justice. Those who take up offices and positions are a different matter.

### What to watch for

**What the principle does and does not deliver.** It grounds an obligation to do your part in a just scheme. It does not certify that any particular instruction is part of your share, and it does not switch off the natural duties, which apply whether or not you have taken anything on. An institution can have a real claim on you and still be asking for something outside it. Keep those two questions apart: *am I bound to this arrangement?* and *is this demand within what I am bound to?*

**The justice condition.** Rawls makes the principle conditional on the scheme being just — though he allows for institutions that are reasonably just in the circumstances rather than requiring perfection. Whether your workplace clears that bar is a real question and this module does not answer it for you. Before you try, settle what you are assessing. The firm? The practice of writing these reports? The wider economic order the firm sits inside? The three can receive different verdicts, and an argument about one of them does not settle another.

**Your own position.** Being born into a country is not an act. Applying for a job, accepting it, and drawing a salary are acts. So the narrowing may place you closer to the case where fair play applies than the ordinary citizen is — which would make Rawls's argument press on you harder, not more gently. Then hold the line from above: even if you are bound, producing a misleading report is not obviously one of the things you took on.

### What it pulls against

Socrates in the *Apology*, who locates the wrong in the act rather than in benefits received.

Arendt, in a particular and limited way. She is not refuting Rawls. She is examining what happens when a person reaches for an obligation as a reason for doing what they are told, and the reaching is a separate matter from whether the obligation is real.

### Questions to carry

- What exactly did you accept when you took the job? Is a salary the benefit of a cooperative scheme, or a price paid for labour?
- Would you accept a fair-play argument from a colleague asking you to cover for them? If not, why does it sound better coming from an institution?
- Suppose the scheme is just and you are fully bound by fair play. Does producing this report fall inside your share, or outside it? What decides that?
- Suppose it is not just. Does that release you from anything owed to your colleagues, or only from Rawls's way of explaining it?

### What kind of argument this is *(read after the text)*

A reciprocity argument. What binds you is generated by your own acts inside a structure rather than by the merits of any particular request. Its strength is that it explains why you owe something to people you never chose and never agreed with. Its difficulty is working out how far the obligation reaches — an account of why you are bound is not yet an account of what you are bound to do.

### Two arguments we do not assign

**Nozick on unchosen benefits.** In *Anarchy, State, and Utopia* (1974), in the chapter section on the principle of fairness, Nozick imagines a neighborhood that assigns each resident one day a year to run a public address system for everyone's entertainment. You have enjoyed 364 days of other people's broadcasts. Are you therefore obliged to take your turn? He says no: benefits conferred on you without your asking cannot by themselves place you under an obligation, or anyone could bind anyone else by doing them unrequested favours. The objection matters here because you did not choose your employer's culture, its reporting conventions, or the colleagues whose budgets now depend on the outcome. You arrived and found them.

We do not assign Nozick because the objection is a single move against a single principle, and it is fully stated above; the chapter around it argues for a political position this module has no occasion to examine.

**Rawls's earlier and stronger version.** A decade before *A Theory of Justice*, in "Legal Obligation and the Duty of Fair Play" (1964), Rawls gave the principle of fairness a considerably larger role in grounding political obligation than the assigned sections give it. The narrowing you have just read is a change of mind. Knowing that is useful: it lets you read §§18–19 as a position he arrived at rather than an obvious place to start, and it shows that the distinction between voluntary acceptance and mere membership is carrying deliberate weight. We do not assign the essay because it is hard to obtain, carries no locators that work across editions, and states a view its author later revised — the assigned sections are where he settled.

## Reading 4: Hannah Arendt, "Personal Responsibility Under Dictatorship"

In *Responsibility and Judgment*, edited by Jerome Kohn (2003). In copyright, and harder to obtain than the others; it has no section numbering, so it is referred to below by its arguments rather than by locator.

### Why it's here

The first three readings assume a person who judges. Arendt examines how people inside a hierarchy stop doing so — not because the occasion for judgment disappears, but because it is evaded, delegated, or redescribed until it no longer looks like a judgment at all. She takes the defenses seriously before taking them apart.

**A caution about register.** Arendt is writing about people who administered mass murder under a totalitarian regime. The comparison to a softened report is ours, not hers. The difference is not only that the wrong is smaller. The conditions differ in ways that change what a person can do: she is describing circumstances of coordinated terror in which the ordinary avenues — objecting, resigning, appealing to someone else — had been closed. You almost certainly have those avenues. What may transfer is the shape of certain excuses. Nothing transfers about the gravity of the acts, and if the comparison begins to feel flattering to your situation, that is worth noticing.

### What to watch for

**The cog argument.** I was one replaceable part of a large machine; it would have run identically without me. Arendt's interest is that this is *true* as a description of how bureaucracies work and still fails as a defense. Work out why before she tells you.

**The lesser-evil argument.** Those who accepted a smaller wrong in order to prevent a larger one, and who often believed it. Watch for two things she adds: that the people making this argument tend to stop noticing that the lesser evil is still an evil, and that accepting small concessions can be the mechanism by which someone is brought to accept larger ones later.

**Her account of the people who refused.** They were not, on her telling, distinguished by better moral theories, superior intelligence, or philosophical sophistication. But they were not thoughtless. What they did was think for themselves about what they would be able to live with, rather than referring the question elsewhere.

**Obedience and support.** She argues that in political life these amount to the same thing, and that the language of obedience lets an adult describe their own participation as something that merely happened to them. Read "support" carefully: it means taking part in an enterprise, not inwardly approving of everything the enterprise does. A person can support something they dislike, which is exactly her point.

### What it pulls against

The Laws in the *Crito*, whose argument is available to any institution whatever, however it behaves.

And the use a reader might make of Rawls — not Rawls's account itself. Distinguish the two. That an obligation can be invoked as a cover is not evidence that it is unreal.

### Questions to carry

- If you soften the report and it is true that someone else would have done it had you refused, what have you done?
- When is the lesser-evil argument correct? It cannot always be wrong, and your manager may be right.
- Is "that's not my call" a description of your position or a decision about it?
- Turn her criterion on itself. "What I can live with" is a fact about you. What stops it from licensing whatever you happen to be comfortable with? What does it tell someone whose conscience is quiet when it should not be? And what does it offer the people affected by the decision, who do not appear in it?

### What kind of argument this is *(read after the text)*

Not a theory of obligation, which is why it reads differently from the other three. It is an account of how judging gets evaded, and an argument that a certain kind of responsibility cannot be transferred to a role or a chain of command. She keeps this sort of responsibility distinct from the responsibility we bear collectively for what is done in our name, which she treats as a different question with different rules.

## Returning to the case

Recall the position you settled on. Before revising it, put the case to each reading in turn.

- **Socrates** asks what the act would do to you, and is unimpressed by what it costs.
- **The Laws** ask what you have taken from this place, and what your colleagues are owed by someone who has taken it.
- **Rawls** asks what you actually accepted, whether the scheme is just enough for the question to arise, and whether this demand falls inside what you took on.
- **Arendt** asks what you are doing, in a sentence with you as its subject.

Whether the *Apology* and the *Crito* genuinely conflict is left open. They may be two answers to one question, or one answer given twice in different circumstances. Decide which, and be able to say why.

### Two questions about yourself

Both, not whichever is more comfortable.

**How might I be wrong to object?** Is the softened version actually misleading, or is it merely more favourable than the version you would have written? Are the findings as significant as you are treating them? What might your manager know about how leadership reads these documents that you do not?

Then make it concrete: name the evidence that would justify withdrawing your objection. If you cannot name any, that is worth knowing about yourself. If you can, say what you would then owe — because a withdrawn objection still leaves you having to produce a report you can defend.

And: do you have an interest in being the person who objected?

**How might my uncertainty be a way of avoiding the cost?** If you chose "information is missing," name the information and say how you would get it. If you cannot get it, say what you will do without it. Not deciding is a decision that the report goes up in someone's version.

Be careful what you are protecting. The objective is a report that is defensible and conduct you can account for. It is not that the final wording is yours. Your manager's version could turn out to satisfy both, and wanting to win is not the same as being right.

### What you would actually do on Monday

Work out the next concrete thing you would do, and what you expect to happen as a result. Not your position — your action. Writing a paragraph helps and is not required.

Hold onto this and your original answer. The case returns in later modules with the facts changed and the stakes raised, and you will want to see what your judgment looked like before you knew where it was going.

## What this module did not settle

Whether the *Apology* and the *Crito* disagree or answer different questions. Whether fair play reaches a demand like this one, and how anyone would tell. Whether Arendt's criterion is a standard or a description of the people who happened to meet it. Whether her cases can be brought this close to ordinary ones without distortion.

The case above is genuinely contested, and reasonable people land differently on it. Not every case in this program is like that. Later modules include situations where what is right is reasonably clear and the entire difficulty is what it costs to do it.
