# Module 1: Conscience and Obligation

*What makes an institutional demand binding, and what could justify refusing one?*

Four readings stage a disagreement. Socrates refuses an official order and explains why. The same man then refuses to escape a sentence he believes is unjust, and explains that too. Rawls sets out what could make an institution's claims on you binding in the first place. Arendt asks what happens to judgment when a role stands between a person and what they are doing.

By the end you should be able to state the strongest case for refusing an institutional demand, the strongest case for honouring obligations to an institution that has wronged you, and what each case requires in order to work. You are not expected to choose between them.

Start with the case below, before the readings.

## The case: the internal report

You are responsible for preparing an internal report. Your manager asks you to remove or soften several unfavorable findings because, in their view, the report is technically accurate but gives senior leadership an unnecessarily negative impression of the team's performance. Nothing you are being asked to write is literally false. You believe the revised report would nevertheless leave a reasonable reader with a materially misleading impression.

Your manager argues that every department presents its work favorably, that leadership already understands how these reports are produced, that your team's budget could be harmed by unnecessary candor, and that several colleagues' jobs may eventually depend on that budget.

You are not being asked to break a law or falsify a number. What do you do?

**Take a position now.** Write it down somewhere you will find it again.

- Comply.
- Refuse.
- Negotiate a middle path.
- Leave, or begin planning to.
- I don't know yet. *(Then: what would you actually do on Monday?)*
- Information is missing. *(Then: what, and could you get it by Friday?)*

## Reading 1: Plato, *Apology*

Freely available; the Stephanus numbers below appear in the margins of most editions. [Jowett translation](https://classics.mit.edu/Plato/apology.html).

### Why it's here

Socrates describes two occasions when he refused an official demand, and his explanation is not the one we expect. He does not say that his conscience forbade it, or that he followed his heart. He says that he knows certain acts to be wrong, that doing wrong damages the person who does it, and that this consideration outranks the danger of refusing.

### What to watch for

The two refusals at 32a–e are not the same act. As a presiding officer he used his position to obstruct an illegal collective trial of the generals, at direct risk to himself. Under the Thirty he was ordered to help arrest Leon of Salamis; he went home instead, and the others went and arrested Leon anyway.

Notice also what Socrates does not claim. He repeatedly denies knowing the large things. His certainty is narrow and concerns particular acts.

And notice his position. He is old, he has no career, and his livelihood does not depend on the people he is defying. He says as much. Whether his argument survives the removal of that independence is a question the rest of the module will press.

### What it pulls against

The *Crito*, immediately and directly. Also Rawls, who will locate obligation in what you have accepted rather than in what an act does to you.

### Questions to carry

- Leon was arrested regardless. Did Socrates' refusal accomplish anything? If it did not, what made it right?
- If refusing is justified, does anything further get owed to the person left exposed?
- Would you make the same refusal if you had children and a mortgage? If not, is that a reason or an excuse?

### What kind of argument this is *(read after the text)*

An agent-centered argument. The wrong is located in what the act does to the person committing it, not in the harm it causes or the rule it breaks. Modern descendants travel under *integrity* and *virtue*. Note what the argument does not do: it does not weigh consequences, and it does not appeal to a principle everyone must follow.

## Reading 2: Plato, *Crito*

Freely available. [Jowett translation](https://classics.mit.edu/Plato/crito.html).

### Why it's here

The man who refused the Thirty now refuses to escape a sentence he regards as unjust. Something survived the wrong done to him. This reading asks what.

### What to watch for

The argument arrives in two stages. First, at 49a–e, Socrates gets Crito to agree that one must never do wrong, not even in return for wrong. Everything after depends on that premise. Test it before conceding it.

Then Socrates does something odd: he stops speaking in his own voice and imagines the Laws of Athens arguing with him (50a–54d). Their speech contains at least four separable claims — that escaping would destroy the city (50a–b), that the relation is one of inequality like that between parent and child (50c–51c), that he was obliged either to persuade the city or obey it (51b–c), and that by remaining in Athens all his life he agreed to its terms (51c–52e).

Take them separately. They are not equally strong, and the weakest ones are stated most confidently. Whether Socrates himself accepts everything the Laws say, or is testing an argument he has put in their mouths, is genuinely disputed.

### What it pulls against

The *Apology*. One possible reconciliation is worth testing: perhaps Socrates refuses to *do* wrong but accepts having wrong *done to him*. See whether the Laws' demands stay inside that distinction. The argument from tacit agreement seems to go further.

### Questions to carry

- Which of the Laws' four arguments would you accept from an employer? Would you accept the parent analogy? The argument from having stayed?
- If you reject all of them and still believe you owe your colleagues something, where does that obligation come from?
- What would have to be true for continued membership to count as agreement?

### What kind of argument this is *(read after the text)*

Obligation grounded in relationship and acceptance rather than in the content of the demand. The claim is that you are bound because of what you have received and what you have gone along with. Rawls makes this precise; Nozick attacks it.

## Reading 3: John Rawls, *A Theory of Justice*, §§18–19

In copyright and widely held in libraries. The section numbers work in both the original and the revised edition, so any copy will do.

### Why it's here

The Laws' agreement argument, stated rigorously and then deliberately narrowed.

Rawls separates two things that ordinary talk runs together. **Obligations** arise from our own voluntary acts. **Natural duties** bind us regardless of anything we have done. The principle of fairness belongs to the first kind: where a just scheme of cooperation exists, and you have voluntarily accepted its benefits or taken up a position within it, you are bound to do your part rather than free-ride on everyone who does.

The narrowing is the interesting part. Rawls holds that most citizens do not acquire political obligation this way at all, because being born somewhere is not a voluntary act. They are bound instead by the natural duty of justice. Those who take up offices and positions are a different matter.

### What to watch for

That narrowing may bind you *more* tightly rather than less. Being born into a country is not an act. Applying for a job, accepting it, and drawing a salary are acts. If Rawls is right about what generates obligation, your situation is closer to the case where fair play does apply than the ordinary citizen's is.

Watch also for the condition he places on the whole argument: the scheme must be just. Your employer is a hierarchy operating in a market and almost certainly does not meet Rawls's standard. Work out whether the principle then applies to you at all, and if it does not, whether anything takes its place.

### What it pulls against

Socrates in the *Apology*, who locates the wrong in the act rather than in benefits received. And Arendt, who will argue that structural accounts of obligation are precisely where personal responsibility gets lost.

### Questions to carry

- What exactly did you accept when you took the job? Is a salary the benefit of a cooperative scheme, or a price paid for labour?
- Would you accept a fair-play argument from a colleague asking you to cover for them? If not, why does it sound better coming from an institution?
- If your workplace is not just, does that release you from anything, or only from Rawls?

### What kind of argument this is *(read after the text)*

A reciprocity argument. Obligation is generated by your own acts inside a structure, and the content of the particular demand does not enter into it. Its strength is that it explains why you owe something to people you never chose. Its weakness is that it can seem to bind you to whatever the structure happens to ask.

### Two arguments we do not assign

**Nozick on unchosen benefits.** In *Anarchy, State, and Utopia* (1974), in the chapter section on the principle of fairness, Nozick imagines a neighborhood that assigns each resident one day a year to run a public address system for everyone's entertainment. You have enjoyed 364 days of other people's broadcasts. Are you therefore obliged to take your turn? He says no: benefits conferred on you without your asking cannot by themselves place you under an obligation, or anyone could bind anyone else by doing them unrequested favours. The objection matters here because you did not choose your employer's culture, its reporting conventions, or the colleagues whose budgets now depend on the outcome. You arrived and found them. Ask what act of yours, if any, does the binding work.

**Rawls's earlier and stronger version.** A decade before *A Theory of Justice*, in "Legal Obligation and the Duty of Fair Play" (1964), Rawls gave fair play a much larger role in grounding political obligation, and he later retreated from it for citizens generally. The retreat is what makes the earlier version worth knowing: if he was right to withdraw the claim for people who are merely born somewhere, the original strong version may still stand for people who applied, accepted, and were paid. Neither text is assigned here — the essay is hard to obtain and carries no locators that work across editions, and what it adds fits in this paragraph.

## Reading 4: Hannah Arendt, "Personal Responsibility Under Dictatorship"

In *Responsibility and Judgment*, edited by Jerome Kohn (2003). In copyright, and harder to obtain than the others; it has no section numbering, so it is referred to below by its arguments rather than by locator.

### Why it's here

The first three readings all assume a person who judges. Arendt examines what happens when role, procedure, and hierarchy remove the occasion for judgment altogether — and she takes the defenses seriously before taking them apart.

A caution about register. Arendt is writing about people who administered mass murder. The comparison to a softened report is ours, not hers, and the difference in stakes is not a technicality. What transfers is the structure of the excuses, not the magnitude of the wrong. If the comparison starts to feel flattering to your situation, that is worth noticing.

### What to watch for

**The cog argument.** I was one replaceable part of a large machine; it would have run identically without me. Arendt's interest is that this is *true* as a description of how bureaucracies work and still fails as a defense. Work out why before she tells you.

**The lesser-evil argument.** Those who accepted a smaller wrong in order to prevent a larger one, and who often believed it. This is your manager's argument, almost exactly: a slightly misleading report now, against a budget cut and colleagues losing jobs later.

**Her account of the people who refused.** They were not, on her telling, distinguished by better moral theories or sharper reasoning. They were the ones who asked what they would be able to live with afterwards.

**Obedience and support.** Her sharpest move for our purposes is the claim that in political life these are the same thing, and that the language of obedience lets an adult describe their own support as something that merely happened to them. Try the substitution on your own case: replace "I was told to" with "I supported it" and see what changes.

### What it pulls against

Rawls, hardest of all. He locates obligation in the structure you participate in; she treats the structure as the place where responsibility disappears. Also the Laws in the *Crito*, whose argument is available to any institution whatever.

### Questions to carry

- If you soften the report and it is true that someone else would have done it had you refused, what have you done?
- When is the lesser-evil argument correct? It cannot always be wrong.
- Is "that's not my call" a description of your position or a decision about it?

### What kind of argument this is *(read after the text)*

Not a theory of obligation at all, which is why it sits last. It is an account of how judgment gets evaded, and an argument that responsibility stays personal because judging is something only a person does. Where Rawls asks what you owe, Arendt asks what you are doing and what you will have been.

## Returning to the case

Find the position you wrote down. Before revising it, put the case to each reading in turn.

- **Socrates** asks what the act would do to you, and is unimpressed by what it costs.
- **The Laws** ask what you have taken from this place, and what your colleagues are owed by someone who has taken it.
- **Rawls** asks what you actually accepted, and whether the scheme is just enough for the question to arise.
- **Arendt** asks what you are doing, in a sentence with you as its subject.

They do not agree, and the disagreement between the *Apology* and the *Crito* is the one to sit with rather than settle. It is recorded in `notes/tensions.md`.

### Two questions about yourself

Both, not whichever is more comfortable.

**How might I be wrong to object?** Is the report misleading, or is it merely less flattering than the one you would have written? Are you certain the findings are as significant as you think? What might your manager know about how leadership reads these documents that you do not? And: do you have an interest in being the person who objected?

**How might my uncertainty be a way of avoiding the cost?** If you chose "information is missing," name the information and say how you would get it. If you cannot get it, say what you will do without it. Not deciding is a decision that the report goes up in someone's version, and it will not be yours.

### What you actually do on Monday

Write one paragraph describing the next concrete thing you would do, and what you expect to happen as a result. Not your position — your action.

Keep both the paragraph and your original answer. The case comes back in later modules, with the facts changed and the stakes raised, and you will want to see what your judgment looked like before you knew where it was going.

## What this module did not settle

Whether the disagreement between the *Apology* and the *Crito* is a real contradiction or two answers to different questions. Whether fair play survives the workplace failing Rawls's justice condition. Whether Arendt's cases can be brought this close to ordinary ones without distortion.

The case above is genuinely contested, and reasonable people land differently on it. Not every case in this program is like that. Later modules include situations where what is right is reasonably clear and the entire difficulty is what it costs to do it.
