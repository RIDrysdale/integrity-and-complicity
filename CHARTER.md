# Charter

## The question

How should a person act with integrity while living or working inside institutions that are compromised, dysfunctional, unjust, or corrupt, especially when leaving is not practical or not desirable?

Most people never face a single dramatic moment of conscience. They face a report they are asked to soften, a colleague they are expected to cover for, a policy they think is unfair, or a culture that slowly shifts what everyone treats as normal. They are rarely sure they are right, rarely free of dependence on the institution, and often benefiting from the very arrangements they question.

This program is for that situation. It is concerned less with moral theory for its own sake than with personal conduct: integrity, complicity, compromise, loyalty, dissent, courage, prudence, responsibility, and knowing when accommodation becomes participation.

Every part of this program answers to those paragraphs. The question's final clause is not decoration. This program is about the person who is stuck: dependent on the institution, implicated in it, often benefiting from the arrangements they object to, and rarely certain they are right. Remove that constraint and this becomes a course on political obligation and civil disobedience. There are many of those already.

## Who it is for

An adult who has encountered the problem at work, in a profession, in government, in a church, in a school, or in a community organization, and who wants to think about it more carefully than the situation allowed at the time.

No prior training in philosophy is assumed. The program aims at university-level understanding. It does not claim to be equivalent to a degree.

## What it is

A sequence of modules, each anchored on a concrete case, each assigning a small number of readings chosen because they disagree with one another. Every reading comes with an account of why it is there, what to watch for, what it pulls against, and questions to carry through it.

The program can be completed entirely by reading and reflection. Written exercises are offered and optional.

## What it is not

- **Not a theory survey.** Theory is encountered through problems and named afterward.
- **Not a course in civil disobedience.** State and law appear, but the center of gravity is ordinary institutional life: the report, the metric, the colleague, the budget.
- **Not a whistleblowing manual.** Dramatic refusal is one option among several, and usually not the first.
- **Not neutral about whether real wrongs exist.** Some institutions are corrupt and some compromises are rotten. Ambiguity is not the house style.
- **Not a program in which the reader is the hero.** The reader is as likely to be implicated, frightened, dependent, or simply mistaken.
- **Not converging on a moral framework.** Disagreements between readings are preserved rather than resolved.

## Editorial authority

Robert Drysdale decides what the program contains. Claude drafts; ChatGPT critiques. Neither model resolves an open editorial question, and neither changes the architecture through a drafting choice. Structural proposals are made explicitly or not at all.

The two models share much of their training data. Agreement between them is not evidence that a judgment is sound.

## Standard of completion

A module is finished when:

1. Its anchor case is written and its readings are assigned.
2. Every assigned reading is obtainable by the reader and cited by a locator that works in any edition.
3. Nothing is quoted that has not been checked against an accessible text.
4. Its readings were chosen for what they contribute to this problem, not for familiarity or for coverage.
5. Robert has read it as a reader and it survived that reading.

**And of the finished program:** traditions outside the Western canon are engaged as full readings where they bear on the problem at hand, and at least one of them was allowed to change something. This is a requirement on the whole, not on each module.
